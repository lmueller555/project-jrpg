# Merovar

**Status:** Approved continental geography and cultural foundations for Auratia.
Existing Eldenmere geography remains the local anchor. Illustrative encounters
remain story seeds; exact political borders and institutions remain open.

**Parent:** [World atlas](../WORLD_ATLAS.md)

Merovar occupies the northwestern part of the world map. Its inhabited
middle latitudes include wooded valleys, mountain uplands, fertile river plains
and a southern maritime coast. Farther north, forests give way to boreal country
and tundra; the far northern coasts form part of the Nivren Crown's
[Rimeward Shores](../POLAR_REGIONS.md), while more detailed inland districts
remain unnamed. Moist westerly winds reach the
Greyfold range from the west, supporting wet slopes and headwater forests. Its
eastern rain shadow supports the drier Redstone March.

The southern shore faces the Amber Sea. The River Leth flows generally south
through the interior into Rookfen and the sea. Eldenmere's brook joins this system
through unnamed tributaries. Its short millrace remains a separate controlled
branch, including the established east-to-west flow beneath the mill wheel.

Aldric's administrative reach includes Elden Vale and nearby upper-Leth
road and quarry districts. It does not establish rule over Merovar. Sovereign
borders, the constitution of the regency and relations between larger states
remain open.

## Elden Vale

[Elden Vale](../regions/ELDEN_VALE.md) is an inland district on the moist western
and southwestern approaches to Greyfold. Mixed woodland, small pastures, gardens
and cultivated terraces surround settlements along its headwaters. Eldenmere's
approved brook descends from northern uplands toward the south; its northern
woodland and Upper Millrace remain immediately local places.

Milling, timber work, wool, orchard produce and household crafts sustain different
parts of the district. Sanctuary service and neighbourly obligations
are visible in Eldenmere, while road settlements have stronger connections to
passing carriers and paid seasonal labour. People disagree about how much work
a household owes its neighbours and what public authorities should provide.
Road repairs and quarry demands can bring those expectations into conflict with
protected ground. A damaged crossing or interrupted delivery gives Caelan a
practical reason to meet those differences through people who need help.

## Greyfold Uplands

Greyfold rises through conifer forest and open moor to alpine meadows and
seasonally snowbound passes. Sheltered valleys support barley plots, livestock
and small settlements; higher routes depend on weather, maintained crossings
and guides. Streams draining its western slopes feed the greener
country below.

Guides, shepherds, quarry workers, woodland households and carriers have distinct
interests. Some communities maintain shared roadside shelters, recording food
and fuel use so the next traveller finds supplies. Others rely on paid inns or
sanctuary hospitality. Felted clothing, stored cheeses and repaired equipment
express long use and practical skill. A visitor's willingness to replenish a
shelter can matter more than impressive titles. Expanding stone extraction may
improve a dangerous road while damaging grazing routes or winter stores.
An escort through a closing pass could reveal those competing needs without
turning mountain residents into a single isolated culture.

Gatherings in sheltered halls can include unaccompanied songs, route stories
and competitive games requiring memory and observation. Woven checks and
embroidered edges distinguish workshops and personal taste; they do not define
every resident's ancestry. Young people can seek training in a river town and
return with fashions their relatives find impressive, irritating or both.

## Lower Leth

Downstream, the Leth spreads through fertile floodplains, wet meadows and orchard
country. Villages sit on higher ground above fields, while larger market towns
grow around ferries, bridges and barge landings. Seasonal flooding replenishes
soil and can also threaten homes and stores.

Landholding varies between communities: leased estates, family plots and shared
fields can coexist along the river. Bargemen, millers, tenant farmers and market
traders experience the same harvest very differently. Weighing houses and public
water-level marks make agreements visible; arguments arise over whose measure
or flood record should be trusted. Local observances may combine remembrance
with shared meals and contributions to households whose harvest failed. An
embankment can protect one settlement while worsening flooding downstream.
The party can first assist an
evacuation or damaged ferry, earning relationships before encountering the
larger disagreement over maintenance and responsibility.

Market days support short comic plays, public music and displays of skilled
weighing, carving or cookery. Prosperous patrons fund performances while local
audiences reward jokes about those same patrons. Schooling and access to legal
advice vary between town households, estate tenants and people moving for work.

## Rookfen

Rookfen follows the lower river through freshwater marshes and branching channels
into a brackish delta. Reed beds, wet woodland, raised islands and silt-rich
farmland form a changing landscape. Seasonal channels can connect communities
that appear separated on a dry-season map.

Fishing households, reed workers, clay gatherers, farmers and trading landings
support several overlapping ways of life. Some settlements maintain raised
walkways; others organise daily travel around boats. Channel markers and shared
landing repairs are matters of ordinary public safety. Coastal families may
mark a remembrance gathering with lamps carried along a safe raised bank,
while inland neighbours favour household visits. Such practices express belief
and memory.
Bank erosion, drainage schemes and shifting shipping routes create arguments
over relocation and compensation. Restoring access to a stranded community
could introduce local expertise, interrupted trade and competing promises
made by distant authorities.

Painted doors, patterned reed screens and movable furnishings give homes strong
individual identities above the wet ground. Boat races and visiting evenings
can join settlements across channels. A wealthy landing and an isolated farm
may share these customs while having very different means to host a gathering.

## Marris Coast

The Marris Coast faces the Amber Sea along Merovar's southern shore. Cliffs,
estuaries, exposed grassland, maritime woodland and sheltered gardens break up
the coastline. Fishing villages and working anchorages sit between larger
ports.

Ship repair, salt production, fishing, warehousing and inland trade create
different neighbourhoods and seasonal movements of labour. Port streets carry
several languages, including those of families whose relatives live across the
sea. Departure songs, letters entrusted to returning crews and meals shared
after a long absence give maritime travel a domestic presence. Temple workers,
craft associations and harbour officials may provide different forms of
assistance. Timber demand and harbour charges can create tension with inland
suppliers and small crews. A delayed vessel, disputed berth or damaged cargo
offers a personal encounter before any larger overseas journey becomes part
of the story.

Public notices and shop signs may use several languages. Harbour comedies and
work songs travel inland with returning relatives, sometimes becoming familiar
long before their origin is remembered. Citizenship, apprenticeship and access
to guild work can determine whether a migrant household feels welcomed or
merely useful.

## Redstone March

East of Greyfold, reduced rainfall supports grassland, thorn scrub and red-soil
uplands, with irrigated pockets along dependable watercourses. Rocky badlands
occupy some exposed districts, while other valleys support prosperous farms.
The name March does not yet define a fixed international frontier.

Permanent farming towns, caravan households, herders and quarry camps share
routes and markets. Cistern maintenance and the recording of water access can
be communal responsibilities in one town and paid services in another.
Hospitality customs may distinguish a traveller's immediate need for drinking
water from the larger supply required by animals or commercial cargo. These
distinctions invite both generosity and argument. New road and quarry contracts
can compete with grazing routes and existing water agreements. A damaged
cistern could force an escort to detour, revealing the difference between a
contractor's promises and the maintenance that residents actually receive.

Shaded markets make room for board games, storytelling and public debate during
rest from work. Some communities value a detailed account of a journey over
grand claims about it. Others are proud of formal titles and inherited
reputations. These differences give neighbours room to judge one another as
individuals within the same broad landscape.

## Story scope and continuity

Elden Vale, nearby Greyfold routes and the upper Leth corridor provide enough
geographic variety for Game I's regional story. Lower Leth, Rookfen, Marris and
Redstone can appear through travellers, goods, letters and personal connections;
visiting every region is not required. Overseas travel remains available to
later stories.

These regions do not determine a person's ancestry, personality, moral position
or magical potential. Borders and institutions will overlap the landscape.
The original sacrificial temple's location remains unassigned. The approved
[companion foundations](../../narrative/game_01/COMPANIONS.md) give Lucan the
Redstone March, Rhea the wooded western Greyfold slopes and Edran the Lower
Leth as home regions; specific settlements remain open.
Regional customs cannot reveal the hidden ancient history or settle
the unresolved mechanics of souls and resurrection.
