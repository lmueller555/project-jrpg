# World and Environment Design Standard

**Status:** Approved central creative design standard. Illustrative concepts are
proposals, not established locations, lore or implemented systems.

**Scope:** Creative principles for designing the world, regions, zones,
settlements, reserves, wilderness areas, dungeons and individual environmental
scenes.

**Purpose:** Create cohesive, thematic and memorable places with distinctive
fantasy identities, believable inhabitants and rewarding exploration.

This is the single maintained reference for the shared creative design process
and place-design brief. Regional documents retain location-specific canon.
[Foundational Lore](FOUNDATIONAL_LORE.md), the [World Atlas](world/WORLD_ATLAS.md)
and the [Revelation Plan](narrative/saga/REVELATION_PLAN.md) retain authority over
established facts, unresolved questions and disclosure limits. The
[Visual Art Standard](VISUAL_ART_STANDARD.md) and
[Terrain Art Standard](TERRAIN_ART_STANDARD.md) retain technical construction,
sizing, rendering and pack-maintenance guidance. Link to those sources instead
of maintaining parallel versions of their rules here.

## 1. Design places players will remember

Every substantial location should offer a recognizable identity and an
experience worth remembering.

That experience might be a spectacular view, an unusual journey, an encounter
with local life, a troubling discovery, a feeling of home or a mystery that
remains with the player.

Before selecting assets, establish:

- What should the player feel here?
- What makes this place distinctive?
- What would someone remember after leaving?
- Why does this place matter to the people who inhabit it?

A region's identity can emerge from landscape, architecture, weather, history,
culture, creatures, sound or the relationships between them. A spectacular
landmark is one useful approach; a distinctive way of life can be equally
memorable.

Beauty, humor, affection, comfort and curiosity belong alongside danger, grief
and grandeur. Give the world emotional range.

## 2. Connect the world at four scales

| Scale | Design responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| **World** | Shared geography, history, supernatural foundations and large-scale relationships. |
| **Region** | A distinctive landscape, climate, cultural context and set of experiences. |
| **Zone** | A particular place that contributes something meaningful to its region. |
| **Scene** | The views, details, actions and discoveries through which players experience that place. |

Related places should share recognizable connections while offering variety.

A farming settlement, an upland sanctuary and a river crossing can belong to
the same region through their materials, water system, trade and customs. Their
architecture, activity, atmosphere and exploration should still reflect their
different purposes.

Connections between regions can appear through changing vegetation, weather,
road construction, goods, clothing, building practices and stories about
neighboring places. Abrupt transitions can also be compelling when geography,
history or an extraordinary event gives them meaning.

## 3. Give each region a distinctive fantasy identity

Describe the region's identity in a short, specific statement before expanding
it.

Useful descriptions connect an extraordinary feature to an experience:

> A high plain where enormous exposed ribs shelter farms, and evening winds turn
> the skeleton into a weather warning heard across the fields.

A weaker description lists ingredients without explaining their relationship:

> A grassy region with bones, ruins, wind and villages.

Develop signature features through several senses and scales. Consider the
distant silhouette, the experience of approaching it, its nearby materials and
sounds, and the small details residents encounter daily.

Select features deliberately. Weather, ruins, giant remains and folklore are
available design tools; every region does not need all of them. Let quieter
landscapes give major spectacles room to register.

## 4. Make extraordinary places feel inhabited

A convincing fantasy environment shows relationships between its features and
its inhabitants.

Ask where people obtain water, food, shelter, materials and access to other
communities. Consider what they maintain, what they have adapted, what requires
specialist knowledge and what they enjoy about living there.

An extraordinary environment may influence:

- Building orientation, construction and maintenance.
- Farming, fishing, gathering and trade.
- Clothing, tools and transport.
- Travel routes, shelters and gathering places.
- Wildlife, vegetation and seasonal activity.
- Customs, celebrations, disputes and local expertise.

Develop the relationships that matter most to the location. A magical feature
need not affect every part of society.

Residents should have individual relationships with their surroundings. One
person may treasure the phenomenon that complicates another person's work.
Familiarity can turn something astonishing to travelers into an ordinary part
of daily life.

## 5. Use weather as a defining part of place

Weather should contribute to a region's character through its appearance,
movement, sound, duration and influence on local life.

Ordinary weather can be memorable: a powerful coastal storm, a valley filled
with morning mist or snow moving across an exposed pass. Fantasy phenomena can
extend that identity through unusual behavior, remarkable scale or unexpected
combinations.

Distinguish between:

| Weather or phenomenon | Design role |
| --- | --- |
| **Regional climate** | Establishes enduring patterns that influence vegetation, settlement and travel. |
| **Recurring local conditions** | Give a place a familiar rhythm, such as daily cliff mist or seasonal flood fog. |
| **Rare meteorological events** | Create anticipation, surprise, celebration or exceptional danger. |
| **Supernatural anomalies** | Introduce distinctive behavior beyond ordinary weather, consistent with established lore. |
| **Aftermath** | Leaves evidence through frost, debris, sediment, altered vegetation or residents' responses. |

Possible phenomena include ascending rain, luminous snowfall, cloud rivers,
unusually persistent storm fronts, colored mists, lightning moving between
distant formations or wind that produces recognizable tones through the
landscape.

These are possibilities for design, not additions to canon.

### Develop an event as an experience

For an important weather event, consider its progression:

1. **Signs:** Changes in wind, animal behavior, distant sound, cloud shape or local preparations.
2. **Arrival:** How the phenomenon enters the player's surroundings.
3. **Peak:** Its defining sight, sound or experience.
4. **Aftermath:** What remains and how activity resumes or changes.

An event need not always be dangerous. It might illuminate a landscape, bring
useful moisture, reveal a view, prompt a celebration or create a brief moment
of extraordinary beauty.

### Let people understand their weather

Show the knowledge residents use: shelters facing away from prevailing winds,
water marks on buildings, collection cloths, storm shutters, seasonal paths,
warning bells or guides reading subtle changes.

Some knowledge can be practical and reliable while the underlying cause
remains mysterious.

### Make defining experiences available

If a phenomenon is central to a region's identity, players should have a
reasonable opportunity to experience it. Use an authored first encounter, a
predictable recurrence, a visible forecast or another clear opportunity.

Specify whether an event is constant, scripted, scheduled, triggered or random.
A design should distinguish intended experiences from implemented systems; it
does not require a global weather simulation.

## 6. Give ancient ruins a present-day significance

Ruins should suggest former purposes, historical change and present
relationships.

Consider:

- What was built here, and what activities did its spaces support?
- What distinguishes its materials, forms and construction?
- What survives, and what changed around it?
- Who inhabits, maintains, studies, dismantles, avoids or claims it now?
- What evidence can players observe without being told its entire history?

Create variety in historical structures. Ruins can be roads, aqueducts,
dwellings, observatories, harbors, gardens, defensive works, industrial sites
or ceremonial spaces.

A partly functioning structure can be especially compelling. A settlement
might still depend on an old bridge, maintain one channel of an immense
waterworks or hold a market beneath a surviving roof.

Distinguish historical layers and builders as they become established. Avoid
automatically attributing every ancient structure to the same civilization.

Architecture should also create spatial experiences: descending through former
streets, crossing the exposed interior of a monumental structure, or
discovering ordinary domestic rooms inside something that first appeared
ceremonial.

Leave room for uncertainty. The original purpose of a structure can remain
unresolved while its current importance is clear.

## 7. Let enormous creatures and their remains reshape places

Living giants, ancient skeletons, petrified bodies and recent carcasses can
establish exceptional scale and a powerful regional identity.

Their presence should influence the surrounding place in selected, meaningful
ways. Remains might become shelter, a route, a boundary, a gathering place, a
nesting habitat or a landmark visible across a region.

### Establish what kind of remains they are

Different conditions create different experiences:

- **Recent remains:** Disruption, scavengers, investigation, mourning or urgent decisions.
- **Ancient bones:** Long adaptation, accumulated stories, weathering and established uses.
- **Petrified remains:** A landscape-scale form whose nature may be disputed or gradually recognized.
- **Incomplete remains:** Suggestive fragments that let players discover the larger shape through exploration.

Preserve a coherent bodily form where recognition matters. Head, spine, ribs,
limbs and joints should relate convincingly to one another even when the
creature's anatomy is fantastical.

### Make scale something players experience

Reveal the landmark through approaches and changing viewpoints. Let nearby
trees, buildings, travelers and wildlife establish its size.

A rib that takes time to walk beneath, a tooth visible above a settlement or
a route following a colossal spine can communicate scale more effectively
than an isolated distant image.

### Develop relationships around the remains

People may use the same remains differently: as heritage, shelter, raw
material, a scientific subject, a sacred place or an obstruction. Those
relationships can support affection, practical cooperation or disagreement.

Great age or size does not establish that a creature's consciousness survives,
that its bones contain usable power or that it connects to the central
catastrophe. Those are separate lore decisions.

## 8. Make folklore part of living culture

Folklore should belong to people who tell, adapt and use it.

Stories may preserve route knowledge, comfort children, explain an unsettling
sound, commemorate loss, justify a claim, accompany a celebration or express
affection for a place.

Represent folklore through more than exposition:

- Place names and travelers' sayings.
- Songs, games and remembered verses.
- Offerings, carvings and household decorations.
- Customs around departure, weather or remembrance.
- Everyday precautions whose explanations differ between people.

Allow variation between generations, occupations, communities and individual
tellers. A guide, a child and a scholar may know different versions of the
same story.

Some folklore can contain truth. Some can be mistaken, symbolic, deliberately
altered or unresolved. It need not always become a quest clue or receive an
authoritative explanation.

Keep three things distinct during design: **what visibly happens, what
characters believe and what the creators have established as true.**

## 9. Connect features selectively

A strong region often develops several expressions of a shared idea.

Weather might shape a ruin's current use. Wind passing through immense bones
might inform travel practices and local stories. A settlement might celebrate
the season outsiders find most intimidating.

Develop the connections that strengthen identity. Other features can have
independent histories, and some mysteries can remain unrelated.

Preserve local significance. Places may matter because people live, work,
gather, play or remember there, without serving the main quest or explaining
the wider crisis.

Thematic depth should emerge through experiences and relationships.
Maintaining, changing, exploiting or relinquishing something extraordinary
can make ideas such as inheritance or responsibility tangible. Quiet pleasure
and beauty can also carry a region's meaning.

## 10. Compose exploration with rhythm

Plan how players encounter a place, not just what appears on its map.

Consider the first impression, the route toward a landmark, glimpsed
destinations, changes in enclosure, optional discoveries, sheltered pauses
and the view back over ground already crossed.

Use changes in scale and detail deliberately. A confined passage can heighten
a broad reveal. A calm inhabited space can deepen the impact of a dangerous
landscape. A small personal detail can make an immense ruin feel connected
to ordinary lives.

Keep important routes, characters and interactions readable through scenery
and effects. Weather and atmosphere should support the intended experience
while respecting the game's visual, audio and reduced-motion standards.

### Design meaningful returns

A later visit can reveal another aspect of a place through weather,
occupation, access, repair, seasonal activity, a changed relationship or
knowledge the player now possesses.

Returning should sometimes allow recognition and reflection, rather than
requiring a new spectacle.

## 11. Preserve consistency while allowing mystery

Established geography, cosmology, history and character knowledge remain
authoritative.

Designers should understand the experience players encounter and the
observations residents can reliably make. A complete scientific or
metaphysical explanation is optional.

Record whether a proposed feature is established canon, a proposal, a local
belief or an unresolved mystery. Approval of this general standard does not
approve every illustrative feature.

Visual effects do not automatically establish combat bonuses, elemental
affinities, hazards or resource rewards. Any intended gameplay effects need
their own explicit design.

For current lore boundaries, consult [Foundational Lore](FOUNDATIONAL_LORE.md),
the [World Atlas](world/WORLD_ATLAS.md), the
[Revelation Plan](narrative/saga/REVELATION_PLAN.md) and relevant regional
documents. In particular, unusual weather or ancient imagery must not quietly
disclose the Aethersphere's hidden purpose or settle unresolved questions
about ancient sites, consciousness and souls.

## 12. Illustrative region concepts

**These examples demonstrate the design method. Their names, locations and
phenomena are proposals only.**

### The Rainward Steps

Along an immense escarpment, rain rises from the valley into an enduring river
of cloud. Ancient stairways disappear upward through the spray.

Settlements hang collection cloths beneath balconies. Guides study changes in
the ascending droplets before taking travelers onto exposed steps. Some
passages offer shelter and quiet conversation; others open suddenly onto the
cloud-filled valley.

One familiar tale says the mountain returns whatever the valley cannot bear
to keep. Different tellers give that saying different meanings.

The region's identity comes from vertical movement, unusual weather,
inherited routes and life adapted to the cliff.

### The Ribwind Plain

The ribs of an enormous dead creature arch above farmland and caravan roads.
Evening winds make different bones sound across the plain.

Experienced carriers recognize approaching weather by which notes begin first.
Villages occupy sheltered spaces beneath the skeleton, while gatherings and
children's games give particular bones familiar local identities.

A proposed road could require cutting through a rib long used as a meeting
place. Stories disagree over whether the creature died protecting the plain
or trying to escape it.

The region combines immense scale with an ordinary, inhabited landscape
whose people have grown up around something extraordinary.

### The Veiled Harbour

A coastal town faces ancient sea walls whose outlines become visible through
a recurring pearl-grey fog.

Residents recognize passages by water striking submerged arches; children
learn those rhythms as clapping games. Fishing families value the fog season,
while some merchants struggle with its delays.

Folklore describes the walls as the harbor of a city still expecting its ships
home. Exploration can sustain that haunting suggestion without confirming a
surviving city or an afterlife.

The region's identity emerges through partial visibility, sound, maritime
knowledge and the continuing presence of an uncertain past.

## 13. Required design brief

Before substantial asset production or implementation for a new or redesigned
place, record:

| Field | Questions to answer |
| --- | --- |
| **Identity** | What is the place's specific, memorable premise? |
| **Emotional intention** | What should players feel, and how might that change? |
| **World connections** | How does it relate to neighboring places and established history? |
| **Geography and ecology** | What supports its landscape, water, vegetation and wildlife? |
| **Inhabitants** | Who lives, works, travels or gathers here? |
| **Signature features** | Which landmarks or phenomena define the experience? |
| **Weather** | What patterns, events, warning signs and aftermath matter? |
| **History** | What visible layers of construction, use and change exist? |
| **Folklore** | What do people say, practice or disagree about? |
| **Exploration** | How do routes, reveals, pauses and discoveries unfold? |
| **Sensory identity** | Which forms, materials, colors, sounds and motions belong here? |
| **Change and return** | What might players experience differently on a later visit? |
| **Canon status** | What is established, proposed, believed or unresolved? |
| **Implementation scope** | What will actually be built and experienced in this pass? |

Scale the brief to the location. Record where a category has little relevance
rather than adding an unnecessary feature to fill it. Keep each place's brief
with its regional/location documentation and link here for the shared method.

Review the design through several lenses: its overview, the player's first
arrival, ordinary traversal, time spent near its defining features and the
perspective of someone who lives there.

The central review question is:

> **Does this feel like a specific place in our world, and does being there
> create an experience worth remembering?**
